## The short answer

Three things change in Swedish schools on 1 August 2026. Most of the attention has gone to the third one.

- **Ordningsregler become skolregler.** In the government's own words, "Dagens ordningsregler ska byta namn till skolregler, bli skolgemensamma och innehålla en plan för konsekvenser vid överträdelser av reglerna." They are renamed, they must be school-wide, and they must contain a plan for consequences when the rules are broken.
- **A förväntansdokument becomes mandatory.** "Skolans förväntningar på eleverna och deras vårdnadshavare ska tydliggöras genom ett förväntansdokument."
- **Schools go mobile-free.** Phones are collected at the start of the day, which we covered separately in [what happens when the phone is the student's support tool](/blog/mobile-free-school-2026-assistive-phone-use).

The phone rule got the headlines. The consequence plan is the one that will quietly decide how the school year goes for students with ADHD, autism and dyslexia, and it is being written right now, in a hurry, in August.

## Why it is being written in a hurry

Skolverket has been commissioned to produce support material for exactly this work, "stöd för skolors och huvudmäns arbete med skolregler, konsekvensplaner och förväntansdokument". That material is due by 1 January 2027.

The rules take effect on 1 August 2026. So there is roughly a school year in which every school in Sweden writes a consequence plan without the national guidance that is being written to help them do it. Whatever a school puts on paper this month is what its staff will apply for that entire period.

## The problem a consequence plan creates for neurodivergent students

The reform's own logic is uniformity. Skolgemensamma rules exist so that the same thing means the same thing in every classroom, which is a genuine improvement for students who are currently subject to twelve different teachers' twelve different tolerances. Predictability is good for neurodivergent students specifically.

The risk sits in the pairing of "school-wide" with "consequences". A consequence ladder is a machine that converts an observed behaviour into a response. It works if the behaviour means what the ladder assumes it means. For a large share of neurodivergent students, it does not.

We have written before about how [behaviour is a signal rather than a character flaw](/blog/trygghet-och-studiero-when-behaviour-is-a-signal). A student who walks out of the room may be avoiding a sensory environment they cannot tolerate. A student who does not start the task may be stuck at the executive step of beginning, not refusing. A student who is late every morning may be in a family where mornings have collapsed. Run each of those through a uniform ladder and the school produces a written warning, then a kvarsittning, then a tillfällig omplacering, for a student whose actual problem is that the support they are entitled to has not arrived.

That is not a hypothetical compliance concern. When a school responds to a manifestation of a disability with escalating consequences instead of adjustments, it is not only failing its duty to give support. It is walking toward [bristande tillgänglighet, which is unlawful discrimination under Diskrimineringslagen](/blog/failing-to-support-npf-student-can-be-discrimination).

## What has not changed, and is easy to forget in August

A consequence plan does not sit on its own. The rest of the Education Act still applies, and three parts of it constrain what the plan may do.

**Proportionality.** Skolverket states it plainly: "En åtgärd måste stå i rimlig proportion till sitt syfte och övriga omständigheter." A plan that prescribes an automatic response, with no room to weigh the individual circumstances, is difficult to reconcile with that sentence.

**Barnets bästa.** "Alla beslut som rör barn under 18 år ska ha barnets bästa som utgångspunkt." This applies to decisions taken under the consequence plan too, not only to decisions labelled as support.

**The duty to investigate.** Repeated difficulty is a trigger for finding out why. The school's obligation to investigate whether a student needs [särskilt stöd](/blog/what-skollagen-requires-extra-anpassningar-sarskilt-stod) is not suspended because the difficulty took the form of a rule violation, and the same is true of the [duty to investigate repeated absence](/blog/school-duty-to-investigate-absence). A consequence and an investigation are not alternatives. A school that applies the first without the second has answered a question it never asked.

There is also a participation point worth raising while the rules are still drafts. Under the current framework, "Ordningsreglerna ska utarbetas, följas upp och vid behov omarbetas under medverkan av eleverna." Student participation is exactly the step where a rule that works for neurodivergent students tends to get written, because the students know which rules they cannot meet and why.

## What to get right before term starts

For rektorer and school leaders writing this now, five things separate a plan that helps from a plan that generates appeals.

1. **Make the first step of the ladder a question, not a punishment.** The most useful consequence plan treats a repeated rule violation as a trigger to check whether the student's support is in place. That single design choice is what keeps the plan on the right side of both the support duty and the discrimination rules.
2. **Write the exceptions down instead of leaving them to the moment.** The mobile rule is the obvious case: assistive use sits [inside the rule rather than outside it](/blog/mobile-free-school-2026-assistive-phone-use), so a school rule that says nothing about it forces staff to improvise at the collection point on the first morning of term.
3. **Say who decides an individual departure, and how fast.** A plan that is uniform in principle still needs a named route for the student whose [åtgärdsprogram](/blog/what-an-atgardsprogram-must-contain) points the other way. If that route takes three weeks, it does not exist.
4. **Write the förväntansdokument for the parent whose child cannot meet a neurotypical expectation.** A document listing expectations of students and guardians reads very differently in a home where the morning routine is already a daily negotiation. Expectations that cannot be met do not produce compliance, they produce shame, and often [absence](/blog/school-duty-to-investigate-absence).
5. **Involve students, and ask the ones who struggle most.** They can tell you which rule they will break by Wednesday, and why, which is more useful than any consultation exercise conducted with the students who were never going to break it.

## Where Nuro fits

The gap this reform exposes is the one we keep meeting. A school can hold both facts, that a student needs a consequence to be predictable and that the same student needs an adaptation to succeed, only if it actually knows what that student's support is, on the day, in the room, without a meeting.

Nuro's job is to make a student's adaptations visible and current, so the person applying the school rules is not working from memory. A consequence plan is safest for neurodivergent students when the school does not have to guess whether the behaviour in front of it is defiance or an unmet need it already documented.
