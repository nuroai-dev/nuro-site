## The short answer

No, and there is Swedish follow-up data on exactly this question. Statistics Sweden (SCB) tracked students who finished year 9 in 1998, 2003, 2008 and 2013, following the 1998 cohort all the way to 2014, to see who later completed gymnasiet, who continued studying, and who became established on the labour market.

The finding that matters most for any school deciding whether to invest in support: **completing gymnasiet mattered more than being eligible for it at 15.** In SCB's words, students who were ineligible for a national programme but completed their studies anyway were established on the labour market at a higher rate than students who had been eligible but did not finish. That held even at the follow-up 16 years after year 9.

Eligibility at 15 is a measurement. Finishing is the outcome. The distance between them is where school support does its work.

## What the data actually says

Two of SCB's headline conclusions are worth quoting plainly, because they point in the same direction:

- Completing gymnasiet gives a higher rate of establishment on the labour market. Among those who were ineligible but completed their studies, a higher share were established than among those who were eligible but did not complete gymnasiet.
- Not completing gymnasiet produces more young people who are neither working nor studying.

Read together, these say something specific. The decisive event is not the grade sheet at the end of year 9. It is whether the student is still there, still enrolled and still finishing, several years later. A student can arrive at gymnasiet behind and still land well. A student can arrive eligible, disengage, and not.

For neurodivergent students this is the whole argument. The risk is rarely that a student with ADHD, autism or dyslexia cannot learn the material. The risk is that the school day becomes unworkable, attendance slips, and the student stops finishing things. [Absence starts earlier and quieter than most schools notice](/blog/school-absence-starts-earlier), and [Sweden does not systematically count it](/blog/sweden-doesnt-count-school-absence), which means the trajectory SCB measured often begins years before anyone records a problem.

## The other end of the same pipeline

The consequences do not stop at the school gate, and a useful illustration arrived from the UK this month. The BBC reported on how neurodivergence is now showing up in workplace disputes: using the Ministry of Justice judgments database, the law firm Irwin Mitchell counted 517 employment tribunal cases mentioning neurodivergent conditions in 2025, up from 265 in 2020, most commonly autism and ADHD. The Ministry of Justice notes those figures should not be treated as official statistics, so read them as a direction of travel rather than a precise count.

The same reporting cites research suggesting the adult population is still substantially underdiagnosed. Work published in The Lancet Regional Health Europe in June put diagnosed adult ADHD in England at around 1.2%, against international prevalence estimates of 3 to 5%. Research from King's College London suggests around 90% of autistic people over 40 remain undiagnosed.

That is the UK, it is about workplaces, and it is about adults, so none of it describes Swedish schools. What transfers is the structure. In both countries the law places a duty on the institution to adapt. In the UK, when an employer skips that duty, the failure can surface years later as a tribunal award. In a Swedish school, when the duty is skipped, the failure surfaces as a student who stops coming, and nobody is ordered to pay anything.

## Why this is a school argument, not a wellbeing argument

Support for neurodivergent students is often discussed as a kindness. SCB's follow-up reframes it as an outcome question with a long tail.

Swedish schools already carry the duty. [Chapter 3 of Skollagen requires extra anpassningar as soon as a student risks not meeting the criteria, and särskilt stöd when that is not enough](/blog/what-skollagen-requires-extra-anpassningar-sarskilt-stod), and [no diagnosis is required to trigger it](/blog/support-without-a-diagnosis-school). The duty is not conditional on how promising the student looks at 15.

What the data adds is the reason the duty is worth resourcing properly. Support that keeps a student engaged enough to finish is not a cost that ends in June. On SCB's numbers it is still visible in the same person's labour market position more than a decade later.

## What this means in practice

Three things follow for a school or a huvudman.

**Do not treat year 9 as a verdict.** A student who leaves without eligibility has not had their outcome fixed. The follow-up work, [lovskola](/blog/lovskola-school-must-offer-holiday-tuition), introduktionsprogram, and adapted support in gymnasiet, is doing more than it usually gets credit for. [Support rights continue at gymnasiet](/blog/npf-support-rights-gymnasium).

**Measure finishing, not just eligibility.** Eligibility rates are easy to report. Completion is what SCB found predicts the later picture. A student quietly drifting toward not finishing is the one to catch.

**Catch the drift early.** The point at which a neurodivergent student is easiest to keep is before the absence pattern hardens. That means noticing which student is struggling with which part of the work, while they are still in the room.

That last part is the capacity problem, and it is what we build for. A teacher can see that a student is slipping. Producing an adapted version of every task, for every student who needs one, week after week, is the part that does not fit in a working day. Nuro exists to make the adaptation the law already requires something a school can actually deliver at scale, early enough to matter.
