On Saturday 14 November, Sweden's finance minister Elisabeth Svantesson (Moderates) spoke publicly about her ADHD for the first time, in an interview on TV4's Nyhetsmorgon. She said she is telling her story because she knows "so many people are struggling and wondering 'how is this going to go?'", and that she wants to reach children and young people above all. (Quotes are our translations from Swedish.)

Her diagnosis came late in life. As a child she was talkative and anxious, and she remembers what it felt like to be told off: "I felt shame when I was told off. I didn't mean any harm." Today she has tools that work, including medication and colleagues who know what she needs. But she also says: "I would have liked to seek help earlier."

The interview came the same week Socialstyrelsen, Sweden's National Board of Health and Welfare, showed that ADHD diagnoses among young people keep rising. It estimates that nearly 14 percent of boys and just over 9 percent of girls aged 10 to 17 had an ADHD diagnosis in 2025. In a class of 25, that is about three students on average. New cases are rising fastest among girls, [the group schools have long missed](/blog/adhd-diagnoses-rising-fastest-among-girls).

It means a lot when someone in one of the country's most demanding jobs says: "I am finance minister, I have the biggest portfolio any finance minister has ever had, and I have ADHD." It shows that ADHD says nothing about what a person can achieve. Svantesson is also honest about what it costs. Anxiety has not stood in her way, but it has made her tired.

There is a lesson for schools in her wish that she had found help earlier. A child does not have to wait for a diagnosis to get support. Under Skollagen, a school must give [extra anpassningar as soon as a need shows](/blog/what-skollagen-requires-extra-anpassningar-sarskilt-stod), and [a diagnosis has never been a requirement](/blog/support-without-a-diagnosis-school). A child who feels shame at being told off needs a school that understands why, and adapts the teaching instead of only correcting the child.

That is why we are building Nuro: so every student gets their schoolwork adapted to their strengths, needs, interests and goals, and fewer have to wait as long as she did.

Thank you to Elisabeth Svantesson for sharing her story.
